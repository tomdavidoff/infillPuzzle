\title{Where does the infill housing go?\\Addition versus division}
\author{Thomas Davidoff, Robert Helsley, and Tsur Somerville\thanks{thomas.davidoff@sauder.ubc.ca; robert.helsley@sauder.ubc.ca; tsur.somerville@sauder.ubc.ca}\\Sauder School of Business, University of British Columbia. \\Updated version is available at:\\ https://github.com/tomdavidoff/infillPuzzle/blob/main/text/main.pdf}
  \maketitle

\onehalfspacing
\begin{abstract}

Several U.S. and Canadian jurisdictions have recently allowed  multifamily homes in formerly single-family-only zones. Within markets, the option to build infill multifamily is commonly exercised disproportionately in cheaper locations. This is puzzling, because the right to add space is more valuable where rents and prices are higher. We distinguish two aspects of zoning allowances: ``addition'' of allowable built space on a fixed lot versus ``division'' of given built space into more homes. Addition is more valuable in better locations; division likely is not. When density cannot vary much with location, richer households, with more willingness to pay for incremental square feet, sort into better locations. Vancouver legalized duplexes in 2018, but allowed them no more space than single family homes. A 2023 reform reduced allowable single family square footage, so that duplexes represented addition and division. Consistent with our model, duplexes initially sorted heavily into cheaper locations and the 2023 reform was associated with a significant spatial reallocation of duplexes towards pricier locations. 
\par\bigskip
\textbf{Keywords:} housing supply and markets, government policy, regulation, state and local government
\end{abstract}

\section{\label{sec:intro}Introduction}

As concerns over housing affordability have grown in political salience over
the last decade, a growing number of U.S. and Canadian jurisdictions have
introduced reforms to allow ``missing middle'', ``infill'', or ``gentle
density'' housing types in formerly single-family-only residential zones. This
housing might take the form of additional density that does not dramatically
change the urban fabric, such as accessory dwelling units (Vancouver's laneway
homes) or ``multiplex'' structures with a range of two to six homes on single
lot, or more noticeable changes in urban form in the missing-middle's densest
form such as row and townhouses or three- to four-storey apartment buildings.
As has been emphasized, e.g. by \textcite{bruecknerSingh} and
\textcite{HsiehMoretti}, restrictive zoning causes deadweight loss most where
real estate is most valuable. It is thus natural to expect that the option to
densify urban land would be exercised most commonly in the neighborhoods where
land and structure prices per unit are highest.

Recent empirical studies have documented that infill housing is, in fact, less
prevalent in the most expensive neighbourhoods in jurisdictions that have
upzoned. We present a parsimonious model of housing type choice by builders
that shows how where redevelopment occurs can depend on the extent to which
policy changes allow the addition of greater density on a lot (``addition'')
and the allowance for more units within that density (``division''). These two
effects may work in opposite directions with respect to the relationship
between infill prevalence and neighbourhood price levels. In the model, when 
infill policies allow more square feet to be built on the same lot when more units are built
(addition), this leads to greater take-up where the price per square foot of
structure net of construction costs is greatest. For a given allowed density,
allowing more (i.e. smaller) units to be created out of the same square footage
(division) will result in negatively sorted take-up if wealthier households
with a greater elasticity of willingness to pay for square footage sort into
more expensive single-family neighbourhoods. Division has more value where
willingness to pay for square footage declines most rapidly in square footage.
That positive sorting of households by income is theoretically likely: in
standard monocentric models affluent households may suburbanize due to flat
bid-rent curves generated by income-elastic demand for housing,\footnote{See
	\textcite{Wheaton77} and earlier work of Muth, Alonso, Mills, and
Beckmann cited therein.} but with single-family zoning leading many households
to a fixed (over-) consumption of lot size, the normality of demand for
location quality should generate positive sorting.

This paper studies patterns of redevelopment from two broad upzonings in
Vancouver, BC, in Canada. In particular, we consider the role of addition
versus division effects in the sorting of building duplexes versus single
family homes on the site of torn down older single detached homes (almost all
small-scale residential development in the affected neighbourhoods, which are
fully built out, comes from demolition of existing homes). The first reform, in
2018 allowed duplexes (division) without an increase in allowable floor area
(addition) in neighbourhoods previously reserved for single detached homes.
That is, duplexes could be built, but with no additional square footage
relative to single detached homes.\footnote{In fact, single family homes,
unlike duplexes under the 2018 reform, are allowed additional floor area
through laneway homes (that can be rented but not sold).} 

The second policy change, in 2023, was more involved, and included a provision
allowing for multiplex homes of up to 8 units on single lots.  Multiplexes
involve addition, as they are allowed more square footage than single or duplex
homes.  Multiplexes have proven relatively unpopular on 33-foot lots, the most
common size in Vancouver, and we treat them as an irrelevant alternative for
estimation purposes. Of more direct interest, the 2023 reform reduced the
amount of square feet allowable for single family structures, so that duplexes
after this reform represent both addition and division. Our model predicts that
prior to 2023, duplexes should have been more prevalent in (relatively!) less
expensive neighbourhoods in Vancouver, generally those on the East Side. The
2023 reform should have made duplexes relatively attractive on the more
expensive West Side by adding an addition effect. 

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. In Section \ref{sec:lit}
we review the literature on upzoning of single detached home neighbourhoods and
particulary the attendant sorting of newly allowed infill housing. Section
\ref{sec:model} presents our model of addition, division, and sorting. We
follow this with a description of the institutional and policy setting in
Section \ref{sec:policies}. Our empirical analysis is in Section
\ref{sec:estimationData}. Section \ref{sec:conclusion} concludes. 

Empirically, we find that Vancouver's low density neighbourhoods are sorted in
that the elasticity of willingness to pay for additional square footage is
greatest where land values are highest. Consistent with our model, duplex
takeup was much greater on the cheaper East Side than the West Side. For
example among single family and duplex redevelopments, in \ewPreYear, \ewDupEastPreYear\% of
redevelopment projects on 33' lots on the East Side were duplexes. On the West
Side it was \ewDupWestPreYear\%. After the 2023 reform an addition effect to duplex
development, the shares have grown and converged to \ewDupEastPost\% on the East Side and
\ewDupWestPost\% on the West Side.

\section{\label{sec:lit}Related Literature}

This paper draws from several different threads of urban economics. The first is an older literature on urban growth under a monocentric model. The second is the literature on residential sorting, and the third is more current work on the effects of land use regulation reforms. Our contribution is to  examine the sensitivity of residential redevelopment to the form of zoning in the presence of households sorting across space. 

Redevelopment more broadly has been long studied in the literature, beginning with the dynamic models of urban growth such as \textcite{Brueckner_1981}, \textcite{HochmanPines_1980}, and \textcite{Wheaton_1982}. With durable but replaceable capital in a monocentric model and no other neighborhood specific amenities, \textcite{Wheaton_1982} finds that redevelopment occurs with substantial increases in the capital-to-land ratio (density and or structure quality) and that, under standard conditions occurs primarily in the urban core. Those papers have no redevelopment frictions caused by local land use policies. Introducing the restrictions as frictions is important as they have been identified by economists as the primary cause of limits on housing supply that increase housing prices (see \textcite{GyourkoMolloy2015} and \textcite{Molloy2020} for comprehensive reviews of this literature).

Over the last decade there have been broad rezonings enacted in cities around the world. The empirical study of the effects of these large scale upzonings on new supply finds support for the positive effect of relaxing density restrictions on redevelopment at higher densities. 

\textcite{Anagol_etal2021} analyze a broad-based 2016 zoning reform in S\~{a}o Paulo, Brazil, that allowed an average increase in the maximum allowed built area ratio (BAR) - analogous to floor area ratio (FAR) or floor space ratio (FSR) - from 1.54 to 2.09. Over six years they note a 66 percent increase in multi-family permit applications for treated blocks compared to the controls (or .012 permits per block per year) resulting in an aggregate 1.9 percent increase in the stock of housing and a 0.5 percent decline in prices.

Like S\~{a}o Paulo, Auckland, NZ also introduced a single broad upzoning. Implemented by the Auckland Council (the government body administering the metro area) in late 2016, the Auckland Unitary Plan (AUP), increased the allowed number of units and density on about 75 percent of the metro area's residential land (about 90 percent of the upzoned area allowed for three units per parcel, with the remaining 10 percent at higher densities). \textcite{GreenawayPhillips2023} identify a large increase in new construction (equal to an estimated 4.1 percent of the existing housing stock over five years) attributable to the policy changes in the AUP. 

Both \textcite{Anagol_etal2021} and \textcite{GreenawayPhillips2023} study relatively short-run effects, within a five-year period following the rezoning. \textcite{BuchlerLutz2024} are able to examine longer-term effects studying Swiss zoning reforms that increased densities over a multi-decade period starting in the 1990s. They study rolling increases in the allowed maximum density (with a median 30\% increase in density) across the Canton of Zurich from 1995 to 2020 using an event study approach for each hectare in Zurich. For a five-ten year post-reform period they find larger effects than in \textcite{Anagol_etal2021}: a 9 percent increase in living space and housing units in up-zoned compared to not up-zoned areas. As well, uptake is greater where existing buildings are already at the maximum allowed density, i.e. zoning is binding. 

While not the entire city, New York, NY introduced a series of neighbourhood-level rezonings affecting approximately 40 percent of the city between 2004 and 2013 (\textcite{Liao2023}). Using a border discontinuity design for local increases in FAR, \textcite{Liao2023} finds an overall 4 percent increase in housing supply over seven years, and 8 percent for areas that were treated with the largest increases in FAR. Using the same zoning reform, \textcite{Peng2023} creates a spatial model with worker residential mobility and finds as a counterfactual to the observed outcomes a lower effect of a 0.7 percent long-run increase in the stock. 

\textcite{Rollet_2025} follows more explicitly in the spirit of models of redevelopment in including patterns of existing development in the assessment of uptake in the upzoning reforms. He creates a dynamic model of the redevelopment choices made by developers within a dynamic spatial equilibrium for households and firms. He finds that the large fixed costs of redevelopment, especially in dense areas, limits the short-run effects of rezoning. In simulations, after 40 years only 18\% of the allowed additional floor space would actually be added.  

In Minneapolis and Portland that enacted similar zoning changes to Vancouver, with the same greater emphasis on division than addition, studies find mixed effects on supply. \textcite{Dong2024a} assesses the Portland, OR upzoning. He finds that as a result of these changes, ``moderate middle'' housing increased from 13.4 percent to 44.7 percent of new building permits in single family zones, but does not provide an estimate of the total change in housing.\textcite{GuMunro_2025} use synthetic controls to test the effects of the Minnesota reforms, finding lower prices and rents, but surprisingly no change in permits.

Our work looks at how sorting can affect rezoning uptake. Starting with \textcite{Tiebout1956} and with the empirical foundation for effects on house prices in \textcite{Rosen1974}, numerous papers have examined sorting by households across space. Sorting on the basis of amenities has been general (\textcite{Bayer_etal2004}), but often in a US context focused on the sorting effects of preferences for school quality. More recent work, as in \textcite{Couture_etal2024} and \textcite{AlmagoDomingues2025}, has extended this to endogenous amenities, such as gentrification. Our model, like that in \textcite{BayerMcMillan2012} focuses on the joint preference for amenities and house type, though in a more focused framing that identifies the effects of the levers of zoning on sorting. 

Several authors have identified geographic patterns of redevelopment activity following zoning reforms. \textcite{MarantzElmendorfKim2023_ADUReforms} find that accessory dwelling unit (ADU) placement in California is widespread, but concentrated in convenient, low- and moderate-rent neighbourhoods, and not in more expensive neighbourhoods. Similar findings are in \textcite{BruecknerThomaz2024}, who find that ADU permitting rates are lower in higher-priced neighbourhoods, even after controlling for income and other factors. Huang et al (2023) analyze the construction of duplexes and triplexes in Seattle following a 2019 upzoning reform and find that these housing types are more likely to be built in lower-priced neighbourhoods. Finally, \textcite{DongHansz_2019} examine the impact of Portland's Residential Infill Project (RIP) on housing construction patterns and find that the new zoning has led to more infill development in lower-priced areas. 

While the work cited above examines upzoning in individual cities, \textcite{Stacy_etal2023} create a cross-city panel to study the effects of relaxing zoning restrictions on new construction more broadly. They conduct newspaper text searches for approximately 180 land use regulatory changes between 2000 and 2019 in eight US metro areas. About half (98) of these lessen restrictions through either allowing ADUs (35) or increasing FARs (15). If they observe a relaxation in any measure, they observe the discrete characteristic of having loosened restrictions is associated with a 0.8 percent increase in residential postal addresses in the jurisdictions that loosen restrictions, when compared to jurisdictions that do not. In terms of geographic patterns, they find the effects come mainly from above-median rent areas.

We study zoning that allows some combination of increased units or allowed floor area on a given lot. With undeveloped lots, an alternative to allowing duplexes would be halving lot sizes. Using data from Atlanta to calibrate a model, \textcite{Ma_2025} estimates the effect of this type of forced upzoning. In her data, the binding constraint is not allowed density, but division, where large lot zoning yields large single family homes in well off areas. In her model she allows for demand heterogeneity in preferences for unit type and locations. Regulatory costs cause developers to build large units on large lots rather than trying to get permission to subdivide and built multiple smaller units. She finds that forcing upzoning results in increased supply as when a single large unit cannot be built, more smaller units are constructed instead. Similarly, \textcite{vonBergmannLauster} find large empirical gains in rare cases of lot-splitting in Vancouver.

\section{\label{sec:model} A model of sorting and density regulations}

A developer who owns a lot of fixed size (one unit of land) chooses how
many of $N$ permitted units to build on the lot. Zoning permits a maximum floor
area ratio (and total square footage due to the unit lot size) of $F(N)$. We
assume, appropriately for Vancouver but not all jurisdictions, that developers
are at a corner solution $F(N)$ for any $N$. Zoning may or may not grant more
square feet total when more units are built. 

Profits from building are given by:

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:profit}
	\pi\left(N,\theta\right) = F(N)x\left(N,\theta\right) + \sigma\epsilon_{iN},
\end{equation}

$F(N)x\left(N,\theta\right)$, where $x(N,\theta)$ is the sale price per square foot
minus average cost per square foot on a lot of exogenous quality $\theta$:

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:x}
	x(N,\theta) = p(N,\theta) - c(N).
\end{equation}

We assume that price, not cost, varies with location quality. The important
content of the assumption for the analysis that follows is that the optimal
choice of building quality can change with $N$, but not systematically with
location. Whether the difference between the optimized cost per square foot of
an $N$plex and that of an $N+1$plex is larger in better or worse locations
strikes us as a second order empirical question.

The additive profit shock $\epsilon_{iN}$ is a developer-specific
additive shock to profits from developing $N$ units. This might map to
differences in fixed costs such as professional fees depending on relative
experience with different unit types (e.g. a developer must pay more for a
duplex-specialist architect or permitting consultant if they do not have
experience with duplexes, but would pay less for single family in which they
are more experienced).

The gain in profits when a city offers a price-taking developer the option to build $N+1$ units at density $F(N+1)$ instead of $N$ at $F(N)$, $F(N+1)x(N+1) -
F(N)x(N,\theta)$ can be written: 

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:Delta}
	\Delta(N,\theta) = \left[F(N+1)-F(N)\right]x(N,\theta) + F(N+1)\left[x(N+1,\theta)-x(N,\theta)\right] + \sigma\left[\epsilon_{iN+1}-\epsilon_{N}\right].
\end{equation}

The first two terms on the right hand side of equation \eqref{eq:Delta}
$\Delta(N,\theta)$ correspond to the addition effect of potentially greater
allowed floor area and the division effect of potentially greater price per
square foot from newly permissible smaller unit types that may be closer to
optimal.

How the gain to taking up the option to build $N+1$ instead of $N$ units varies with location is determined by: 

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:compStatic}
	\frac{\partial \Delta(N,\theta)}{\partial \theta} = \left[F(N+1)-F(N)\right]\frac{\partial x(N,\theta)}{\partial \theta} + F(N+1)\left[\frac{\partial x(N+1,\theta)}{\partial \theta}-\frac{\partial x(N,\theta)}{\partial \theta}\right].
\end{equation}

The first term on the right hand side of \eqref{eq:compStatic} is positive as
long as $F(N+1)-F(N)>0$. The second term, the change in the division effect,
will be negative if the residents of less expensive neighbourhoods have less
willingness to pay for the benefits of larger units and more privacy embedded
in $N$plexes versus $N+1$plexes.

Whether willingness to pay for size and privacy increases in neighbourhood
quality is an empirical question, but monocentric city models provide an
intuition for why the condition would likely hold. Bid rent curves for location
are the ratio of willingness to pay for incremental location divided by
home size. With the denominator shut down by zoning constraints because only
single family homes of common size exist, households with greater willingness
to pay for location (presumably those with higher incomes) live in the better
locations. If the change in housing quality from upzoning is small relative to
willingness to pay differentials, the sorting pattern should not vary enough
with rezoning to change neighbourhood sorting dramatically. However, both
because high income households may sort into single family homes in worse
locations, and because the dollar value of homes is larger and subject to more
variation with the same elasticity in better locations, the sign of
\eqref{eq:compStatic} is empirical. 

For example, the following scenario admits parameters under which duplexes
dominate in a cheaper neighbourhood and single family homes in the more
expensive neighbourhood and other parameters in which the opposite is true.
Suppose households with income $y$ have utility over single family homes
($N=1$) and duplexes ($N=2$) on the East Side $(\theta=1)$ and West Side
$(\theta=2)$ given by $U = 3-N+\theta + \log(y-p(N,\theta))$. The city is open
and has a fixed supply of single family homes, some of which may be split in
half in either neighbourhood at no cost and converted into two duplex units.
Outside utility is $\bar{u} = k+\log(y-p_{0})$. The price of any of the four
location-type pairs is determined by the highest bid among all income types.
Single family homes give way to duplexes when the price of duplex is more than
half that of a single family home. Whether and where single family homes are
divided into duplexes depends on the income distribution and the
characteristics of the outside option.\footnote{With two income levels
(invariant to whether in the city or in the outside location), 3 and 10, and
$k=3, p_{0}=2.5$, the East Side has duplexes and the West Side has single
family homes. If everyone has income $10$, $k=2$, $p_{0}=1$, there are single
family homes in the East and duplexes in the West.}

To take the model to data, the additive cost shock interpretation of
$\sigma\epsilon_{iN}$ is useful. The probability that $N+1$ units are more
profitable than $N$ units is:

\begin{equation}
	Pr(\Delta(N,\theta) > 0) = \Lambda\left(\frac{\Delta(N,\theta)}{\sigma}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\Lambda$ is the logistic cumulative distribution function. 

The effect of the 2023 rezoning on the relationship between local land prices and the probability that a developer will choose to build $N+1$ (2) units for sale instead of $N=1$, should be captured with an interaction between neighbourhood price level (prior to rezoning) and an indicator for post-2023. The logit model with underlying extreme value distribution renders the presence of the option to build multiplexes irrelevant to the single family versus duplex.


\section{\label{sec:policies} Institutional background}

Vancouver, British Columbia, is a Canadian city with a population of 662,248 as
of the 2021 Canadian census. The city has been densifying over time. In 2001, the City had 65,390 single detached homes, 39,580 ``other ground oriented homes such as townhomes and half-duplexes, and 131,130 apartments. By 2021, the counts were 44,755 single family, 70,650 other ground oriented, and 189,940 apartments.\footnote{2018 and 2025 Metro Vancouver Housing Data Book.} The loss of single family homes is attributable to a near absence of unbuilt residentially zoned land.

This discussion of zoning changes draws on several City of Vancouver documents.\footnote{\textcite{CoV2004Suites,CoV2019SuitesMemo,CoV2018LanewayReview,CoV2018DuplexReport,CoV2018DuplexAdmin,CoVRS1Schedule2023,CoV2023MissingMiddle,CoVR1Schedule}.} Vancouver has historically zoned most of its residential land for single family homes, with different variants of ``RS'' zoning. As of 2018, before duplexes were permitted in RS zones, the assessment roll records \duplexBldgStockEighteen{} duplex buildings in the City (\duplexFolioStockEighteen{} assessment folios, as each strata duplex unit is a separate folio), spread across \duplexZoningCodesEighteen{} zoning codes. \duplexTopFiveZoneShareEighteen\% were in the five most common of these (\duplexTopFiveZonesEighteen), \duplexRTShareEighteen\% were in the two-family RT zones, and only \duplexRSShareEighteen\% were in RS zones per the BC Assessement data described below. Vancouver incrementally formally allowed rental suites in basements of single detached homes starting in 1986 in a restricted set of zones, with all RS zones allowed suites by \suitesAllRSYear{}. The large majority of suites are believed not to conform with regulations: the City estimated that \suitesIllegalShareTwoThousandFour\% of suites in areas where suites could be legalized remained unauthorized in 2004, and later that permitted suites were only \suitesPermittedShareTwentyNineteen\% of an estimated \suitesTotalTwentyNineteen{} in 2019. After basement suites, the city next moved to allow laneway homes, with the RS-1 and RS-5 zones (\lanewayFirstLotShare\% of single family lots) enabled in \lanewayFirstYear{}  and the remaining RS zones in \lanewayAllRSYear{}.

In \duplexCouncilMonth{} 2018, duplex homes were approved by council in nearly all RS zones, with the by-law enacted on \duplexEnactedDate{}. Generally, duplexes were allowed the same FSR as a single family home (with or without a suite): \rsDuplexFSR{}, against \rsSingleBaseFSR{} for a single detached house, rising to \rsSingleMaxFSR{} when above-grade floor area was limited. Duplexes were not permitted an additional laneway home on the same lot. In that way, a single family home with a laneway was granted more total square footage than a duplex, so that the value to a builder would come from dividing the purchaseable square footage into two units (laneway homes cannot be sold separately from the main structure).

A zoning reform approved by council in \rOneCouncilMonth{} 2023 and effective \rOneEffectiveDate{} had several components. First, all RS zones were combined into a single R1-1 zone. RT zones were not included, although multiplexes were later extended to some RT zones in \rOneMultiplexRTYear{}, per \textcite{vonBergmannMultiplex2025}. Second, the maximum FSR for single family homes was set at \rOneSingleFSR{}, with no more conditional increase to \rsSingleMaxFSR{}, and for duplexes at \rOneDuplexFSR{} \parencite{CoVR1Schedule}. Also, multiplexes of up to 4 units were allowed on lots with at least \rOneFourUnitFrontageFt{}  of frontage and \rOneFourUnitAreaFt{} square feet of area. That includes the most commonly sized lots (33 feet wide and 122 feet deep), and indeed any 33-foot lot over \rOneFourUnitDepthThirtyThreeFt{} feet deep. Up to \rOneSixUnitMax{} strata units (\rOneRentalUnitMax{} if all are secured rental) were allowed on lots with at least \rOneSixUnitFrontageFt{} feet of frontage and \rOneSixUnitAreaM{} \rOneSixUnitAreaFt{} square feet; 50 feet wide is another common width, particularly south of 16th Avenue. Multiplexes were allowed a base FSR of \rOneMultiplexBaseFSR{}, rising to \rOneMultiplexMaxFSR{} for secured rental or with a density bonus contribution.

\subsection{Testable hypothesis}

The pure division effect of the 2018 allowance of duplex homes suggests that duplex homes should have been more prevalent in less expensive neighbourhoods (concentrated on the East Side, see \textcite{SanghoonDaryl}) with (relatively) less affluent buyers than in more expensive neighbourhoods. The 2023 reforms added an addition effect to building duplexes, as $F(2)>F(1)$ after 2023. This suggests a difference in difference analysis: duplexes should have been relatively more attractive than single family homes after 2023 in more expensive neighbourhoods. 

This suggests a difference-in-differences structure with a dependent variable measuring whether a building permit is for a duplex or single family home and the right hand side interaction of interest price level times a dummy for the permit being issued after the 2023 reform. The natural universe is permits granted after the 2018 reform in R1. Confining the analysis to single family and duplex permits after the 2023 reform allowed multiplexes requires an irrelevance of independent alternatives assumption. These considerations are discussed in section \ref{sec:estimationData}  

\section{\label{sec:estimationData}Estimation and data}

We wish to estimate the effect of the 2023 reform on the relationship between neighbourhood price levels and the relative take-up of duplexes versus single family homes conditional on redevelopment.

\subsection{Transaction and assessment data from B.C. Assessment}

From B.C. Assessment we have transaction-level data that includes sale price and date of transaction. These sales data are merged with assessment data from 2018 that describes the type of home, location (and hence census tract and neighbourhood), square footage in the main structure, the year built and the ``effective'' year based on permitted improvements and observable building condition. A caveat to the effective year and year built is that for older transactions, the building may not yet have been subject to renovations made as of the date (2018) at which the observables are identified.\footnote{For example, a sale in 2014 that is recorded in the 2018 data may have had an effective year of 1984, but if the owner updated or tore down and rebuilt in 2015, the 2018 data would indicate a more recent effective year.} We use effective year rather than year built because retention of a foundation may be sufficient to retain the original year built of an otherwise new structure.

To estimate a price per square foot at the census tract or neighbourhood level, we take the local coefficients for areas $j$ from a regression of the form:

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:ppsfHedonic}
	\log \text{ppsf}_{ijt} = \alpha + \sum_{t}\beta_{t}I_{t} + \sum_{e}\delta_{e}I_{e} + \sum_{j}\gamma_{j}I_{j} + \epsilon_{ijt}
\end{equation}

ppsf is the price per square foot (main building total built area). $I_{t}$ are sale year indicators and $I_{e}$ are effective year indicators, with effective years up to \ppsfEffBinYear{} (the bottom \ppsfEffBinShare\% of sales) pooled. Because the effective year is recorded as of 2018 assessment, we drop the \ppsfAgeNonPos{} sales (\ppsfAgeNonPosShare\%) with an effective year after the transaction year, homes renovated after they sold. We estimate these for single family transactions in zones now R1 from \ppsfFirstYear{} until council approved duplexes in RS zones in \duplexCouncilMonth{} \ppsfLastYear{}, so that estimates predate the duplex period and are not affected by relative duplex supply post-2018. Generally, it will be older buildings with smaller square footage than the maximum FSR allowable on redevelopment. For newer buildings, there is clustering near the corner solution of maximum allowable FSR.

The estimates use \ppsfSalesTract{} sales in \ppsfNTract{} tracts and \ppsfSalesNbhd{} sales in \ppsfNNbhd{} neighbourhoods with at least \ppsfMinObs{} sales each. The effective-year control has almost no effect on the local price per square foot estimates: the correlation between the two sets is \ppsfCorEffTract{} across tracts and \ppsfCorEffNbhd{} across neighbourhoods. Mean price per square foot is \ppsfWestPremiumTract\% higher in West Side than East Side tracts (the East Side is defined as east of Ontario Avenue).

\subsubsection{Elasticity of price with respect to square footage or lot size and the single-duplex premium}

To evaluate the assumption that the gap in willingness to pay for a single family home versus a duplex grows in local price per square foot, we estimate the elasticity of price with respect to square footage for each tract and neighbourhood. We add an area-specific slope on log square footage to equation \eqref{eq:ppsfHedonic}, using the same \ppsfFirstYear{} to \duplexCouncilMonth{} \ppsfLastYear{} single family sales on 33' lots now zoned R1 \parencite{BCAssessment2019}. The mean elasticity is \elasMeanTract{} across tracts and \elasMeanNbhd{} across neighbourhoods. \subsection{Regression analysis}

That difference-in-differences is complicated by the presence of a third alternative, multiplexes, newly allowed in 2023. Assuming that errors in log profits have a type 1 extreme value distribution in \eqref{eq:profit}, then the relative appeal of duplexes versus single family homes is unaffected by the presence of the multiplex option. We thus include in our analysis on the logit as well as OLS difference in differences. Either version requires parallel trends: absent the 2023 reform, the relationship between neighbourhood prices and duplex take-up would have continued on the path it followed between the two reforms. Whether multiplexes, granted more FSR, but further divided relative to duplexes, would be more or less attractive on more expensive lots is unclear.


To test whether the 2023 reform led to an increase in the share of duplex permits among R1 duplex and single family permits in more expensive neighbourhoods, we first estimate tract- and neighbourhood level difference in differences in aggregated neighbourhood or tract shares:
 
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:ShareDiD}
	\frac{\text{Duplex}_{jt}}{\text{Duplex + Single}_{jt}} = \alpha + \beta_{1} \text{Post 2023} \times \text{ppsf}_{j} + \beta_{2}D_{T} + \beta_{3}D_{j} + \epsilon_{ijt}
\end{equation}

Table \ref{tab:ShareDiD} shows the results of estimating \eqref{eq:ShareDiD} at the tract and neighbourhood levels for new-build single family and duplex permits on 33' lots now zoned R1 \parencite{COVdata,BCAssessment2019}. Periods $t$ are twelve-month windows of application date relative to the 2023 reform, $ppsf_{j}$ is the local log price per square foot from \eqref{eq:ppsfHedonic}, and cells are weighted by their number of permits, so that the estimates equal those of a permit-level linear probability model with the same fixed effects. The coefficient on the interaction term is positive and statistically significant: \sdTractB{} (standard error \sdTractSE) across \sdNTract{} tracts and \sdNbhdB{} (\sdNbhdSE) across \sdNNbhd{} neighbourhoods, indicating that the 2023 reform was associated with an increase in the share of duplex permits in more expensive neighbourhoods. Scaled by the West minus East gap in mean log price per square foot (\sdGapTract{} across tracts, \sdGapNbhd{} across neighbourhoods), the estimates imply a relative increase in the West Side duplex share of \sdEffectGapTract{} and \sdEffectGapNbhd{} percentage points. Standard errors are clustered at the tract and neighbourhood levels, respectively. 

There are \sdNNbhd{} neighbourhoods with R1 zoning and enough sales to estimate a price level, a small number of clusters for cluster-robust inference per \textcite{CameronGelbachMiller2008,CameronMiller2015}, so we also report wild cluster restricted bootstrap $p$-values with the weights of \textcite{Webb2023}: \sdTractP{} for tracts and \sdNbhdP{} for neighbourhoods. Columns 2 and 5 add $ppsf_{j}$ times a linear period trend, as there has been convergence in pricing over time between the East and West Sides, per \textcite{SanghoonDaryl}. The trend coefficients are close to zero (there is not a trend towards convergence separate from the discrete jump), and the interaction coefficients are similar in magnitude (\sdTractTrendB{} and \sdNbhdTrendB) but less precisely estimated (\sdTractTrendP{} and \sdNbhdTrendP). The base specification includes the entire period from the September 2018 duplex approval through November 2026; because permits applied for but not yet issued are not in the data, the last period is incomplete. Columns 3 and 6 narrow the window to 6 months on either side of the 2023 reform. Only \sdWinPermits{} permits in tracts with permits on both sides of the reform date remain, and the estimates are positive but imprecise (\sdTractWinB, \sdTractWinP{} for tracts; \sdNbhdWinB, \sdNbhdWinP{} for neighbourhoods). We find that changing the period over which tract permit shares are aggregated to months or quarters has approximately no effect on point estimates.

Table \ref{tab:LogitDiD} estimates the analogous difference in differences at the lot level. The outcome is an indicator that a new-build permit is for a duplex rather than a single family home:

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:LogitDiD}
	\Pr(\text{Duplex}_{ijt} = 1) = \Lambda\left(\alpha + \beta_{1} Post 2023 \times ppsf_{j} + \beta_{2}D_{t} + \beta_{3}D_{j}\right),
\end{equation}

where $\Lambda$ is the logistic function. The logit coefficient on the interaction is \lgTractFE{} (standard error \lgTractFESE) with tract fixed effects and \lgNbhdFE{} (\lgNbhdFESE) with neighbourhood fixed effects. At the West minus East gap in log price per square foot, these imply that the reform multiplied the odds of a duplex on the West Side relative to the East Side by \lgTractOR{} and \lgNbhdOR, close to the unconditional ratio of odds ratios of \ewOddsRatio{} above. 

We present standard errors under different schemes to handle problems of small samples and spatial correlation. First, fixed effects estimated in small samples generate bias per, e.g. \textcite{NeymanScott1948}. There are an mean of approximately \lgPermitsPerTract{} permits per tract and \lgPermitsPerNbhd{} per neighbourhood. We thus present test statistics using a wild bootstrap. We also present lot-level results using a linear probability model and a model in conditional logit model in which a control for price per square foot replaces tract dummies (this is also done in specifications (2) and (4)) for the LPM and logit. We include standard errors clustered by tract or neighbourhood and alternative Conley standard errors to account for (strong) spatial correlation across neighbourhoods.

All of the analysis above considers the decision of what to build conditional on a tear-down. A different question left to future research is whether the upzoning increased the pace of tear-downs relative to a suitable benchmark. 

\section{\label{sec:conclusion}Conclusion}

A fairly general model of housing density choice by a developer suggests competing effects in the spatial allocation of infill housing. The opportunity to divide the same space into more units will be more valuable where willingness to pay for incremental square footage is less. Classical monocentric city models of residential sorting with limited density choice suggest this will be in relatively inexpensive areas. On the other hand, if building more units is associated with the relaxation of binding zoning constraints, infill should be relatively likely in higher quality locations where land is more expensive. Data from Vancouver support all three of these predictions. The elasticity of home prices with respect to size is very highly correlated with average price per square foot across neighbourhoods. The choice to build a new duplex rather than a single family home was highly sorted into the inexpensive east side when zoning was division only, but equalized across locations after allowable single family size was shrunk in 2023.
